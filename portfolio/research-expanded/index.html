Understanding the Invisible System
The coordinator is the system — but the system doesn't exist in any tool. I spent 3 weeks studying how coordinators actually work, what patients actually feel, and where the gaps hide.

Coordinator Interview Notes
5 sessions · 127 min total
C1
Senior Coordinator · 6 yrs Theme: Knowledge Silos
I keep everything in my WeChat favorites — doctors' phone numbers, which department does what, prep instructions for each test. If I lose my phone, I lose my job.
Personal messaging app as de facto knowledge base. Zero institutional documentation.
C2
Junior Coordinator · 8 mo Theme: Onboarding Gap
When I started, the senior just said 'follow me for a week.' There's no manual. Every patient type has a different process and I just try to remember.
Knowledge transfer is purely oral/shadow-based. 4–6 week ramp-up, high error rate during transition.
C3
Patient Service Lead · 4 yrs Theme: Cross-Hospital Friction
We had a patient who needed an MRI at the public hospital. I called 6 times before someone picked up. Then they told me the patient needs a referral letter in Chinese. I had to come back, translate it, scan it, send it back. The whole morning was gone.
Single referral = 6+ phone calls, 3+ system switches, 2+ hours. Requirements discovered reactively, not proactively.
C4
Bilingual Coordinator · 3 yrs Theme: Workflow Opacity
The hardest part isn't the language. It's knowing what the patient should do next. They ask me 'what happens after the scan?' and I have to call the doctor, wait, call back the patient. If I could just see the whole path, I wouldn't be the bottleneck.
Coordinator spends most time as information router, not translator. Full journey invisible until each step is manually clarified.
C5
Night Shift Coordinator · 2 yrs Theme: Continuity Gap
At night it's just me. If a patient has an emergency question about their appointment tomorrow, I have to dig through chat history to find what we discussed. There's no single place where the patient's journey is recorded.
Single point of failure on night shifts. Patient context scattered across WeChat, CRM, and personal memory.
Patient Interviews
3 sessions · retrospective
P1 · German, prenatal care High Anxiety
I didn't even know I needed to fast before the glucose test. Nobody told me until I arrived and they said they couldn't do it that day. I had taken a half-day off work for nothing.
P2 · American, orthopedic referral Lost & Confused
After the X-ray, the doctor said 'go to the second building, third floor, room 305.' I got lost for 20 minutes. There were no English signs. I was in pain and just stood in the hallway.
P3 · French, pediatric Relief → Anxiety
The coordinator was amazing — she called the hospital, arranged everything, and sent me a message. But the next day when I had a question, she was off. Nobody else knew about my case.
On-Site Observation Log
3 days · 2 hospitals

Key Insights
3 insights · evidence-based
1
The coordinator IS the system.
Patients rely entirely on coordinators to translate, route, and remember every step. When the coordinator is unavailable, the journey stalls.
Evidence: "I keep everything in WeChat" (C1) · "No manual, just follow me" (C2) · "No single place where journey is recorded" (C5) · Night shift = single point of failure (Obs)
Implication: The system exists in people's heads. To scale, we must externalize it — without removing the human.
2
80% of the journey is predictable — the 20% defines success.
Same referral types follow similar steps. But edge cases (abnormal results, insurance changes, hospital-specific requirements) are where coordinators add the most value — and where current tools fail hardest.
Evidence: OB-GYN referral follows same 8-step pattern (C1, C4) · MRI requirements discovered reactively (C3) · Glucose test prep never communicated (P1) · 8.5 min per booking × predictable steps (Obs)
Implication: Automate the predictable 80% with reusable templates. Design flexible tools for the 20% that requires human judgment.
3
Patients don't need a portal — they need a guide.
Under stress, long instructions become noise. Patients don't want to navigate a healthcare portal; they want to know the single right next action, delivered calmly.
Evidence: "Just tell me what to do next" (P3) · 22 min lost in hospital (P2) · Glucose test miscommunication (P1) · Emotional curve hits crisis at booking + visit stages (Journey Map)
Implication: Reduce patient-facing complexity to absolute minimum — one action at a time, contextually timed, in their language.
Ideation — 4 Concept Directions Explored
Divergent → Convergent
✕ Rejected
Smart Checklist
A digital checklist app for coordinators to track referral steps.
Too simple. Doesn't solve cross-hospital coordination, knowledge sharing, or patient-facing guidance. Misses the system-level problem.
✕ Rejected
Patient Self-Service Portal
Let patients book appointments and navigate hospitals themselves.
Medical appointments require clinical judgment. Patients under stress can't self-navigate. Removes coordinator's value. Conflicts with Insight 3.
✕ Rejected
Unified Hospital API
Connect all hospitals' booking and records systems into one platform.
Technically infeasible short-term. Hospitals won't open APIs. Scope too large for individual project. Doesn't solve the coordinator's workflow problem.
✓ Selected
Task Chain Engine + Dual Interface
A rule-based task chain engine powering a coordinator desktop platform (B2B) and a minimal patient app (B2C).
Addresses all 4 themes: externalizes knowledge (T1), handles cross-hospital unpredictability (T2), supports routing (T3), gives patients one next step (T4). Keeps human in control.
↪ How Might We
Help care coordinators manage complex cross-hospital journeys with a structured, reusable task-chain system — while still giving patients clear, calm, human-centered guidance at every step?
①
Task-First
Organize information by patient task path, not hospital department structure.
②
Continuity
Every step carries context, status, and a clear next action across hospital boundaries.
③
Human-in-the-Loop
Automate repetitive friction, but keep coordinators in control of critical decisions.
